A good workshop is organised around the hands, not the eyes. The tools that are used every hour hang within reach of the bench; the ones used once a week live in the drawer below; the ones used once a year can go on the high shelf where a step is needed to fetch them. Visitors sometimes remark that the place looks untidy, because the neat rows of chisels they expect from magazine photographs are nowhere to be seen. The woodworker who owns it does not mind. Tidiness, she says, is for people who do not work here.

She learned the trade from an uncle who made church furniture and never once wrote down a measurement. He held the piece in his hands, looked at the space it would fill, and cut. Occasionally he was wrong and a joint had to be recut, but far less often than his nephew and niece, who measured everything twice and still managed to leave a gap. She has since made her peace with the tape measure, but she keeps his habit of pausing before every cut, blade lifted, to check that the grain runs the way she expects. That pause has saved more boards than any ruler.

The orders that come in are mostly repairs now: a chair leg here, a drawer runner there, the occasional table that has been in a family for a century and has finally begun to rock. She likes the repairs more than the new commissions. A new piece is a conversation with the customer; a repair is a conversation with whoever made the original, and the old makers were very good at what they did. Every joint she opens teaches her something about how the work used to be done, and she takes those lessons back to her own pieces, so that the tradition continues without anyone quite deciding that it should.
