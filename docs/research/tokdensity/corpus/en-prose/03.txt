The committee met for the fourth time that month to discuss the same agenda item, and by now everyone in the room could recite the arguments on both sides without looking at their notes. The proposal was simple enough on paper: move the archive from the basement of the old town hall to a rented unit on the industrial estate, where the humidity could be controlled and the boxes would not sit within reach of the river when it flooded. The objections were equally simple. The rent would cost eleven thousand a year, the volunteers who catalogued the collection were mostly over seventy and did not drive, and the town hall basement, whatever its faults, was where the archive had always been.

The chair, a retired teacher with a gift for letting silences run long enough to become uncomfortable, asked whether anyone had actually visited the industrial estate. Two people had. One described it as bleak but practical; the other said it smelled of diesel and that she would not want to spend her Tuesday afternoons there. The treasurer pointed out that the insurance company had written twice about the flood risk and that a third letter might come with a premium increase or a refusal to renew. The secretary noted that the last flood had been in 2009 and that the water had stopped four steps short of the archive door.

In the end they voted, as committees do, to form a subcommittee. It would visit the unit, cost out a minibus for the volunteers, and report back in the autumn. Everyone agreed this was sensible. Everyone also knew that the autumn meeting would look very much like this one, and that the boxes would spend another winter in the basement, four steps above the high-water mark, waiting for a decision that nobody was quite prepared to make.
