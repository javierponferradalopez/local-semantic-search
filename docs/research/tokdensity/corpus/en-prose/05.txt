The lighthouse keeper's log for the winter of 1931 survives in the county archive, and it makes for oddly compelling reading. Most entries are a single line: wind north-west, force six, lamp lit at 16:40, extinguished at 07:15. Every few pages, however, something happens. A steamer passes too close to the reef and the keeper fires a rocket to warn her off. A gull breaks a pane in the lantern room and he spends the night with cardboard over the gap, relighting the wick each time the draught snuffs it. His wife falls ill in February and the relief boat cannot land for nine days because of the swell; he records her temperature twice a day in the margin, in a smaller hand, as if the log were not quite the right place for it.

What strikes the modern reader is the absence of complaint. The keeper notes the weather, the state of the lamp, the ships that pass and the supplies that arrive, and he does so with the same flat attention whether the day was uneventful or terrifying. The only time his voice rises is on the twenty-third of March, when the relief boat finally lands and his wife is taken ashore. That entry reads, in full: "Boat landed 11:20. M. away to hospital. Lamp lit 18:05." The next day he is back to wind and visibility.

The archive keeps the log in a climate-controlled box and lets researchers handle it with cotton gloves. A local historian has transcribed it and posted the text online, where it has found a small but devoted audience of people who read a page each evening, the way others read a psalm. They say it calms them. Something about a man doing his job carefully, night after night, in the dark, with no expectation that anyone would ever read about it, turns out to be exactly what a certain kind of reader needs.
