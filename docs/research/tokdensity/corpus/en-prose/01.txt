The harbour town had lived from the sea for as long as anyone could remember, and the rhythm of the tides still set the pace of its days. Boats went out before dawn and came back in the early afternoon, their holds heavy with mackerel and the occasional lobster, and the fishmongers on the quay opened their stalls the moment the first hull touched the wooden pier. Tourists who arrived in summer found the whole scene charming and photographed it endlessly, but the fishermen themselves had little patience for cameras and even less for questions about how the season was going.

The season, in truth, was going badly. Quotas had tightened for the third year in a row, fuel prices had doubled since the spring, and two of the larger boats had been sold to a company on the other side of the bay that ran them with hired crews and sent the catch straight to the city by refrigerated lorry. The remaining owners met on Thursday evenings in the back room of the Anchor, where they drank slowly and talked about grants, cooperatives and the possibility of selling directly to restaurants. Nothing was decided, but the meetings continued because stopping them would have felt like giving up.

The harbour master, a quiet woman who had inherited the job from her father, kept a ledger of every arrival and departure in a leather-bound book that the council had long ago asked her to replace with a spreadsheet. She refused, politely and repeatedly, on the grounds that the book had never crashed, never needed a password and could be read by candlelight when the power went out in a storm, which happened at least twice a winter. The council eventually stopped asking. The book, she said, would outlast all of them.
