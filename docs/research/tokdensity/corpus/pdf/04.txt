Chapter 7
The Long Winter
The snow came early that year, in the last week of October, and by the
middle of November the road to the valley was closed for good. The vil-
lage settled into the routine it had followed for as long as anyone could
remember: the animals were brought into the lower barns, the woodpiles
were checked and re-checked, and the schoolteacher moved her lessons in-
to the back room of the inn, where the great stove kept the children
warm enough to hold a pencil.
Marta's grandfather had told her that the winters of his own childhood
had been longer and harder, and she had believed him without quite under-
standing what he meant. Now, watching the drifts climb past the window-
sills, she began to understand. A hard winter was not a matter of tempera-
ture. It was a matter of time: of the days that stretched between one
piece of news and the next, of the weeks in which nothing happened ex-
cept the slow diminishment of the flour sack and the equally slow accumu-
lation of small grievances between people who could not get away from
one another.
Her mother dealt with it by working. There was always something: mend-
ing, preserving, the endless business of keeping the house warm and the
family fed. Her father dealt with it by silence, sitting by the stove
with his pipe unlit and his eyes on the fire, and answering questions a
full minute after they were asked. Marta herself dealt with it by read-
ing, which the household regarded as a mild eccentricity, tolerated be-
cause it kept her quiet and out of the way.
The book that winter was an atlas, borrowed from the schoolteacher and
returned every Friday so that it could be borrowed again on Monday. She
learned the rivers of Africa and the railways of Russia, and she traced
with her finger the routes that ships took from Lisbon to Buenos Aires,
and she wondered what it was like to live in a place where the road was
never closed. When the thaw finally came, in the second week of April,
she found that she was almost sorry. The world was about to get large
again, and she had grown used to the smallness.
