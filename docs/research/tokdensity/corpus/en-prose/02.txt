Most people who take up running as adults do so for reasons that have little to do with running itself. They want to lose weight, or sleep better, or prove something to a version of themselves that gave up on sport at fourteen. The first weeks are miserable in a way that no article about the joys of the morning run ever admits: the lungs burn, the knees complain, and the mind spends every minute negotiating an earlier finish. Then, somewhere around the sixth week, something shifts. The distance that once felt impossible becomes the warm-up, and the runner starts to notice the light on the river, the smell of the bakery on the corner, the particular sound of their own footsteps on wet pavement.

That shift is what keeps people going long after the original reason has faded. The weight may or may not come off; the sleep may or may not improve. But the run becomes a fixed point in the day, a half hour in which nobody can ask for anything and the only decision that matters is whether to turn left or right at the end of the street. Runners who have done it for years describe it less as exercise than as a kind of maintenance, like brushing teeth or taking out the rubbish: not always pleasant, but unthinkable to skip.

The clubs that form around this habit are among the most democratic institutions in any town. Nobody cares what you do for a living or how fast you are; the only currency is showing up. A surgeon and a warehouse worker can run side by side for an hour and talk about nothing but blisters and the weather, and both will go home feeling that they have had a good conversation. It is a small thing, and it is also, for many of them, the best part of the week.
